## How the loop plays, based on advertised features

The core loop, as described, is short: position a ragdoll, apply force, observe the physics, repeat. The "huge drop" is the headline setup, and the bone-breaking goal gives each attempt a score-like purpose. Breaking "all" of your bones implies a finite, countable set of bones per ragdoll, though the official page does not state a number.

What makes a ragdoll sandbox work is consistency. You want the same launch to produce roughly the same result so you can learn from failures. You also want enough variance that repeated attempts stay interesting. Physics engines handle that tension naturally, and a ragdoll simulation leans on it heavily. That is why these games reward patience more than reflexes: you are testing a system, not fighting an opponent.

## Tips that follow from the advertised design

Since the game is a physics simulation, the most useful habits are about control and observation rather than memorized inputs. The official page does not document hotkeys or control schemes, so any specific key bindings you read elsewhere should be checked in game.

- Let a launch finish before judging it. Physics simulations resolve over time, and a result that looks like a failure mid-flight can end in a full break.
- Change one variable at a time. If you adjust drop height, angle, and ragdoll choice all at once, you cannot tell which change mattered.
- Watch the ground, not the ragdoll. The surface and landing point usually determine the outcome more than the pose in the air.
- Use short sessions to compare ragdolls. Once you unlock more than one, run the same setup on each so the comparison is fair.
- Expect server variation. With up to 20 players per server, physics behavior can differ slightly from a solo server. If a result seems inconsistent, note it rather than assuming you made a mistake.

None of these tips guarantee success. They are reasoning based on how physics sandboxes generally behave, not on documented mechanics for this specific game.
